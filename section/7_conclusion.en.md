# Conclusion
In this document, we addressed three AI usage patterns—applications with built-in AI functionality, applications using an external LLM, and applications using agentic AI—and applied to them three threat modeling techniques: STRIDE, STRIDE+AI, and MAESTRO. The threat modeling was carried out by three teams, each consisting of three members, and was performed a total of nine times.

As a result, in threat modeling, rather than evaluating only the threats specific to AI in isolation, evaluating them in an integrated manner that also includes existing system threats makes it possible to grasp the threats to the system as a whole more comprehensively. Also, from the perspective of the documents required for threat modeling, when performing threat analysis that goes into the internal workings of the AI, it helps to have documents that go into further depth in addition to documents such as the DFD. Furthermore, from the perspective of the knowledge required of the participants who carry out threat modeling, it is desirable for participants from different standpoints, such as the red team and the blue team, to take part and to discuss from diverse viewpoints; on the other hand, when performing an analysis focused on AI, it is important to include at least one participant with specialized knowledge of AI security. Alternatively, it is also effective to provide classroom training or the like for acquiring basic knowledge of AI threats before carrying out the threat modeling. Note, however, that MAESTRO is a threat modeling technique intended for multi-agent systems, so this point needs to be taken into account.

This document also publishes information on the threat models addressed and their results. We hope that this information will serve as a reference when conducting threat modeling.
